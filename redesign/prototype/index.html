Between physics
and the sky.

I’m Mingjie Jian, an astrophysicist reading stellar spectra. I study what they reveal, from individual stars to the Milky Way, and how far we can trust the physics used to interpret them.

Research Associate · Institute of Astronomy, University of Cambridge

The question

How well do we really understand the light from stars?

Every stellar abundance is an inference through a model: an atmosphere, a line list, a treatment of line formation. When a survey observes four million stars, any weakness in that chain is repeated four million times.

My work goes after the chain itself — testing its physics against high‑quality spectra, building the software that carries it, and using it to study stars directly.

Research

From atoms to surveys.

Three connected layers of the same problem: the physics that shapes a spectrum, the tools that model it at scale, and the stars it lets us read.

I
Line‑formation physics

The physics of the spectrum

Are our atomic and atmospheric models good enough for the spectra we now observe? High‑order Balmer lines test how hydrogen’s Rydberg levels dissolve in a stellar plasma — a direct check on the Hummer–Mihalas occupation‑probability formalism.

Software

PySME — Spectroscopy Made Easy, in Python.

A spectrum‑synthesis and fitting code for stellar parameters and abundances, built on the SME radiative‑transfer core. I lead its current development, with a focus on survey‑scale performance and on physics that can be validated.

  • 01Line‑list preselection and dynamic line lists for very large line data
  • 02NLTE departure coefficients, implementation and validation
  • 03Line broadening, instrumental and rotational convolution
  • 04Parallel synthesis for survey pipelines
